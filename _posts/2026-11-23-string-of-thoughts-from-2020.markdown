The humanisation of alien nature through religion; the alienation of human invention through mechanisation; art as the middle term, which liberates the aesthetic effect from iconography: the human revealed at last as useless and arbitrary (Manet’s Déjeuner). The Renaissance as perhaps the true “middle age” in which iconography, religious impulse, and the nascent aesthetic sensibility held in balance, before the isolation and predomination of the third. Valéry’s nostalgia for the “total artist,” or universalising intelligence. Manifestoism as convulsion of the aesthetic sensibility (proliferation and propagandisation of isolated “effects”).  The Cantos: the grab-bag as a possible means of unifying “effects”: vortex a figure for the internal organisation of associated effects — rarely achieved as over-dependant on private knowledge, and misguided musical analogy: success in parts, failure on the whole. The contemporary condition of total formlessness held together by advances in technological power; the dream that technology might come to progress itself (“artificial intelligence”) or absolute inutility (Agamben’s Arcady, yet ego et); the ancient condition of a unity conferred passively from without. From pointless action (magic) to appease the invented personae of external forces to the force of externalised invention rendering all action pointless (liberty). All activity essentially inconsequential (one can do whatever because whatever one does doesn’t matter). Human autonomy an illusion of technological force. The force of private love perhaps the only consolation.
